## Abstract

Modern enterprise computer networks face unprecedented volumes of sophisticated cyber threats, ranging from distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) and reconnaissance probes to application-layer remote code execution exploits. Traditional Signature-based Intrusion Detection Systems (SIDS) suffer from high false-negative rates when encountering zero-day vulnerabilities or polymorphism. Conversely, while conventional Machine Learning (ML) classifiers offer strong pattern recognition capabilities, their widespread operational adoption within enterprise Security Operations Centers (SOC) remains constrained by the "black-box" dilemma—security analysts cannot easily discern *why* a given packet stream triggered a critical alert.

This research presents **IntelliTwin AI**, a production-grade cyber threat intelligence platform that harmonizes supervised and unsupervised machine learning, game-theoretic Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), and large language model (LLM) reasoning. In contrast to earlier academic prototypes that relied on synthetic or leaked datasets, this platform is trained and evaluated on an authentic stratified partition of **25,000 network flow records** from the benchmark **UNSW-NB15** dataset generated by the Australian Centre for Cyber Security (ACCS). 

The platform benchmarks Extreme Gradient Boosting (**XGBoost**), **Random Forest**, and an unsupervised **Isolation Forest** baseline. The XGBoost classifier emerges as the champion architecture, achieving a **94.95% (±0.0031) 5-fold cross-validation accuracy** and a **94.48% held-out test accuracy** with **0.9430 precision**, **0.9780 recall**, an **F1-score of 0.9602**, **0.9901 ROC-AUC**, and **0.9954 PR-AUC**. Using **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)** TreeExplainer, granular local feature attributions are extracted to quantify the marginal contribution of individual packet attributes (e.g., packet rate, byte ratios, and connection windows). These statistical attributions, coupled with raw telemetry, are orchestrated into **Google Gemini**, synthesizing automated Tier-3 SOC incident reports mapped to the MITRE ATT&CK framework with concrete remediation playbooks. The report provides a full forensic analysis of why prior works and prototypes inadvertently report artificial 100% accuracies due to synthetic fallback generators, demonstrating an evidence-based, reproducible, and verifiable cyber defense workflow.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background & Motivation
The proliferation of cloud computing, microservices, and Internet of Things (IoT) devices has exponentially expanded enterprise attack surfaces. Security Operations Centers (SOCs) are inundated with hundreds of thousands of network flow logs daily. This alert volume leads to acute alert fatigue, delayed breach containment, and severe analyst burnout.

Traditional Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) rely primarily on static rule matching (e.g., Snort, Suricata). While computationally efficient, these systems fail to generalize against novel attack vectors. Machine Learning has emerged as a promising alternative; however, operational deployment introduces three critical challenges:
1. **Model Opacity**: Deep neural networks and ensemble trees do not natively yield explainable justifications for triage.
2. **Evaluation Biases & Synthetic Leakage**: Many published academic prototypes report artificial 100% accuracies caused by synthetic or non-overlapping synthetic datasets, which collapse when deployed on real network traffic.
3. **Actionability Gap**: Standard ML classifiers merely output a scalar probability (`0` or `1`) without offering tactical containment guidance.

### 1.2 Research Objectives
The primary objectives of this capstone research are:
1. To engineer a reproducible ML pipeline trained on authentic benchmark flow data (**UNSW-NB15**) with strict train/test isolation, zero preprocessing data leakage, and rigorous 5-fold cross-validation.
2. To integrate game-theoretic Explainable AI (**SHAP TreeExplainer**) to decompose black-box ensemble decisions into interpretable marginal feature contributions for security analysts.
3. To develop an autonomous GenAI SOC Copilot leveraging **Google Gemini** to synthesize actionable Tier-3 incident response reports mapped to the MITRE ATT&CK matrix.
4. To build a hardened, production-ready, full-stack application with strict Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), token-bucket rate limiting, structured observability, and zero-crash fallback resilience.

## 2. Literature Review

The development of network intrusion detection systems has progressed across three distinct epochs:

### 2.1 Classical Signature & Rule-Based Systems
Early intrusion detection systems relied on exact pattern matching against byte signatures of known malware (Roesch, 1999). While establishing negligible false-positive rates for established exploits, these approaches prove ineffective against zero-day exploits, protocol obfuscation, and encryption.

### 2.2 Machine Learning & Anomaly Detection on Benchmark Datasets
The introduction of benchmark datasets such as KDD Cup 99, NSL-KDD, and UNSW-NB15 catalyzed machine learning research in anomaly detection:
- **Moustafa & Slay (2015)** established the **UNSW-NB15** dataset to rectify the synthetic biases, redundant records, and outdated traffic dynamics of KDD99. Using the IXIA PerfectStorm tool, ACCS simulated realistic low-footprint attack categories (Fuzzers, Analysis, Backdoors, DoS, Exploits, Generic, Reconnaissance, Shellcode, Worms) alongside contemporary normal enterprise traffic.
- **Chen & Guestrin (2016)** introduced **XGBoost**, demonstrating that sparsity-aware tree learning and second-order gradient boosting consistently outperform neural networks and standard decision trees on high-dimensional tabular data.
- **Breiman (2001)** established **Random Forests**, demonstrating that bagging decorrelated decision trees reduces model variance and provides robust resistance to feature noise.
- **Liu, Ting, & Zhou (2008)** proved that anomalies are "few and different," demonstrating that recursive random partitioning in **Isolation Forests** can isolate outliers near the root of tree structures without requiring labeled data.

### 2.3 Explainable AI (XAI) & Generative AI in Cybersecurity
While ensemble trees provide superior empirical accuracy, their non-linear decision boundaries resist human interpretation:
- **Lundberg & Lee (2017)** established **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**, unifying cooperative game theory (Shapley, 1953) with local surrogate models. In cybersecurity, SHAP enables analysts to inspect the exact feature pushes (e.g., whether TTL, source loss, or inter-arrival time triggered the alert).
- **Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs)** demonstrate high proficiency in synthesizing technical context, mapping telemetry to MITRE ATT&CK techniques, and drafting containment playbooks (Brown et al., 2020).

## 3. Dataset Provenance & Methodology

### 3.1 UNSW-NB15 Dataset Provenance
This research utilizes the authentic benchmark **UNSW-NB15** dataset created by the Australian Centre for Cyber Security (ACCS). The raw benchmark dataset comprises:
- `UNSW_NB15_training-set.csv`: 32,293,018 bytes, SHA-256: `bec7dd5ec88dc2a0ccc7a07879d338395ed7421750f675fd0339e07dfe0648fa`
- `UNSW_NB15_testing-set.csv`: 15,380,800 bytes, SHA-256: `734fe6642edf758f7c94d7d9149426b49d202fe8e7bf0bef47392489c3c0a559`

### 3.2 Stratified Partitioning & Zero Leakage Protocol
From the authentic benchmark, an auditable stratified partition of **25,000 network flows** was sampled:
- **Training Set**: 20,000 flow records (80%)
- **Held-Out Test Set**: 5,000 flow records (20%)
- **Class Distribution**: 68.32% attack flows (17,080 / 25,000) and 31.68% normal flows (7,920 / 25,000).

**Zero Data Leakage Enforcement**:
To eliminate preprocessing data leakage, the transformation pipeline (`ColumnTransformer`) was fit **strictly on the 20,000 training records**:
1. Continuous features (`dur`, `sbytes`, `dbytes`, `rate`, `sttl`, etc.) were scaled using `RobustScaler` (interquartile range scaling resistant to volumetric attack spikes).
2. Categorical features (`proto`, `state`) were transformed using `OrdinalEncoder` with unknown token handling (`-1`).
3. The held-out 5,000 test set was transformed using the frozen preprocessor parameters.

### 3.3 Forensic Analysis of the "100% Accuracy" Anomaly
A key contribution of this project is the forensic resolution of the 100% accuracy claim seen in prior prototypes:
- In earlier versions of `download_unsw.py`, when legacy UNSW mirror URLs returned HTTP 404, the script defaulted to an internal synthetic fallback generator.
- The fallback generator sampled features using disjoint Poisson distributions (`np.where(labels == 0, np.random.poisson(lam=10), np.random.poisson(lam=100))`).
- Because the normal and attack distributions had zero overlap, classifiers trivially achieved 100.0% accuracy.
- When evaluated against authentic UNSW-NB15 network flows with realistic feature overlap, the authentic test accuracy is **94.48% (XGBoost)** and **94.02% (Random Forest)**, establishing true scientific validity.

## 4. Mathematical Formulations & Algorithms

### 4.1 Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)
XGBoost minimizes a regularized objective function combining convex loss and tree complexity:

$$\mathcal{L}^{(t)} = \sum_{i=1}^n l\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(x_i)\right) + \Omega(f_t)$$

where the regularization term $\Omega(f_t)$ penalizes tree depth $T$ and leaf weights $w$:

$$\Omega(f_t) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^T w_j^2$$

Using second-order Taylor expansion:

$$\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \frac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \Omega(f_t)$$

where the first and second order gradients are:
$$g_i = \partial_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}} l(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)}), \quad h_i = \partial_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}}^2 l(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)})$$

### 4.2 Random Forest Classifier
Random Forest constructs an ensemble of $B$ bootstrap-aggregated decision trees $\{T_b\}_{b=1}^B$. For each split node in tree $T_b$, a random subset of $m \approx \sqrt{M}$ features is evaluated using the Gini Impurity criterion:

$$I_G(p) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^K p_k^2$$

where $p_k$ is the proportion of samples belonging to class $k \in \{0, 1\}$. Final classification is obtained via majority ensemble voting:

$$\hat{y} = \text{mode}\left(\{T_b(x)\}_{b=1}^B\right)$$

### 4.3 Isolation Forest (Unsupervised Baseline)
Isolation Forest detects outliers by randomly selecting a feature and a split value between the feature's minimum and maximum. The anomaly score $s(x, n)$ for an instance $x$ over $n$ samples is given by:

$$s(x, n) = 2^{-\frac{\mathbb{E}(h(x))}{c(n)}}$$

where $h(x)$ is the path length of $x$ in an isolation tree, and $c(n)$ is the average path length of unsuccessful searches in a Binary Search Tree (BST):

$$c(n) = 2 \ln(n - 1) + 0.5772156649\ (\text{Euler's constant}) - \frac{2(n - 1)}{n}$$

When $s \to 1$, $x$ is classified as an anomaly.

### 4.4 SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
To allocate fair attribution $\phi_i$ to each network feature $i \in F$, SHAP leverages the classic Shapley value from cooperative game theory:

$$\phi_i(v) = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \left[ v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S) \right]$$

where $v(S)$ represents the expected model prediction conditional on the subset of features $S$.

### 4.5 Risk Calibration & Uncertainty Formulation
Raw probability $p = P(\text{Anomaly})$ is transformed into a calibrated integer risk score:

$$\text{RiskScore} = \text{round}(p \times 100, 1)$$

Epistemic uncertainty is quantified via normalized probability entropy:

$$U(p) = 1.0 - 2 \cdot |p - 0.5| \in [0.0, 1.0]$$

## 7. Explainable AI & GenAI SOC Incident Response

When an anomalous packet is submitted (e.g., volumetric SYN flood):
1. **ML Model**: Evaluates $P(\text{ANOMALY}) = 0.994$, assigns Risk Score $99.4/100$, Severity `CRITICAL`, Uncertainty $0.01$.
2. **SHAP TreeExplainer**: Flags `rate` ($\phi = +0.284$) and `ct_dst_src_ltm` ($\phi = +0.191$) as the dominant drivers of the risk elevation, while `dur` ($\phi = -0.042$) slightly attenuates the score.
3. **Google Gemini LLM**: Ingests sanitized telemetry and SHAP vectors, producing:
   - **MITRE ATT&CK**: Mapped to `T1498` (Network Denial of Service) and `T1498.001` (Direct Flood).
   - **Investigation Steps**: Direct instructions for examining firewall connection tables and inspecting source spoofing.
   - **Mitigation Playbook**: Explicit kernel tuning directives (`sysctl -w net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies=1`) and perimeter rate-limiting rules.
4. **Autonomous Fallback**: If Gemini credentials are unconfigured or rate-limited, the system transparently serves a deterministic rule-based triage report without failing.

## 8. Limitations & Threats to Validity

1. **Encrypted Payload Inspection**: The system relies on flow-level statistical metadata (packet counts, inter-arrival times, window sizes). When TLS 1.3 with Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) is deployed, deep payload inspection is unfeasible.
2. **Adversarial Flow Perturbation**: Sophisticated adversaries could inject micro-delays to suppress calculated packet rates, evading tree splits tuned on rate features.
3. **Class Imbalance in Benchmark**: The UNSW-NB15 dataset contains 68% attack flows, reflecting high-density lab simulations. In typical enterprise production, attacks constitute $< 0.1\%$ of total flows.

---

## 9. Ethical Considerations

1. **Dual-Use Risk**: Model feature importance insights could theoretically inform attackers on which flow parameters to manipulate to evade detection.
2. **Privacy Compliance**: Network telemetry contains IP addresses. IntelliTwin AI implements data minimization and does not store unhashed payload data.
3. **Hallucination Prevention**: GenAI outputs are explicitly distinguished from verified ML telemetry; LLM-suggested MITRE ATT&CK codes are presented as analytical hypotheses, not confirmed attribution.
